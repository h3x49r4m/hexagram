Reinforcement Learning Algorithms

2026-06-14

Reinforcement Learning (RL) is the field dedicated to teaching agents how to behave in an environment to maximize cumulative reward. While the overall goal—making an agent act optimally — seems simple, the algorithms required to achieve it are deeply complex.

Foundational Conceptual Axes

To understand how a powerful algorithm like DQN or SARSA works, we must first understand the three foundational conceptual axes that define the field:

  • Goal (Prediction vs. Control)
  • Data Source (On-Policy vs. Off-Policy), and
  • Computational Engine (Monte Carlo vs. Temporal Difference).

I. The Goal: Prediction vs. Control

This axis asks: Are we assessing value, or are we optimizing action?

On-Policy and Off-Policy Learning

2026-06-14

If Prediction and Control define the goal of Reinforcement Learning (the destination), then On-Policy and Off-Policy define the rules of the road (the source of the data). This distinction is perhaps the most mathematically sophisticated and practically important concept in advanced RL, determining whether your learning process is stable, efficient, or entirely misleading.

At its core, the difference is simple: Where does the data you use to train the algorithm come from?

RL Prediction and Control

2026-06-14

In the field of Reinforcement Learning (RL), every algorithm, every dataset, and every research paper aims to solve a problem. While many algorithms seem to do the same thing — learn what is “good” — they are fundamentally attempting to answer two distinct, yet related, questions:

  1. Prediction: “If I follow this specific set of rules, what will the average outcome be?”
  2. Control: “What should my set of rules be, so that the outcome will be the absolute best possible?”

These are the two primary objectives of RL. They represent the difference between assessment and optimization, and understanding this distinction is key to mastering the field.

Monte Carlo Methods

2026-06-14

The term “Monte Carlo” comes from Statistics and Probability Theory.

At its heart, a Monte Carlo method is an ingenious computational technique that allows us to estimate a numerical result by relying entirely on repeated random sampling.

Instead of attempting to solve a massive, intractable equation (an analytical solution), the core idea is elegantly simple: If we run a random process thousands or millions of times and calculate the average result, that average will converge to the true expected value.
