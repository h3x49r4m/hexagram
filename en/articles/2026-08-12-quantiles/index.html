Quantiles

📅 August 12, 2026 • ⏱️ 13 min read
🏷️ Tags: Quantiles Statistics Distributions

In the vast landscape of data analysis, practitioners often default to calculating the mean. While the arithmetic average is a foundational statistic, relying solely on the mean can be perilous. The mean is acutely sensitive to outliers and fails miserably when data distributions are asymmetrical or possess extremely heavy tails.

The solution lies in a concept far more robust and informative: the quantile.

This article explores what quantiles are, why they are essential tools in understanding data spread, and how their behavior changes dramatically when analyzing key distributions—the Gaussian (Normal), the Uniform, and the Cauchy.

What is a Quantile?

At its core, a quantile is simply a measure of position. It tells you the value below which a certain percentage of observations in a dataset fall.

Definition and Formula

Given a probability distribution $X$, and a percentile $p$ (ranging from 0 to 1), the quantile $Q_p$ is the value such that the probability of the variable being less than or equal to $Q_p$ is $p$.

Mathematically, for a given dataset $D$ of size $N$:

$$ Q_p = \text{The value } x \text{ such that } P(X \le x) \ge p $$

Common Terminology:

  • The 50th percentile is the Median (the 50th quantile, $Q_{0.50}$).
  • The 25th percentile is the First Quartile ($Q_{0.25}$).
  • The 75th percentile is the Third Quartile ($Q_{0.75}$).

Calculation Example (Empirical Data)

When dealing with a small, finite dataset, we use the sorted sample data to calculate empirical quantiles.

Example: Consider a sample dataset $D = \{10, 12, 15, 20, 33\}$. (Sample size $N=5$).

Goal: Find the 25th percentile ($Q_{0.25}$).

Step 1: Sort the Data (Already done): $D_{sorted} = \{10, 12, 15, 20, 33\}$.

Step 2: Calculate the Index (L): The index $L$ for the desired percentile $p$ is given by:

$$L = p \cdot (N - 1) + 1$$

Step 3: Calculate $L$ for $p=0.25$:

$$L = 0.25 \cdot (5 - 1) + 1 = 1.0 + 1 = 2$$

Step 4: Find the Quantile: The 2nd position in the sorted list is the quantile.

$$Q_{0.25} = 12$$

Robustness Measure: Interquartile Range (IQR)

The most common measure derived from quantiles is the Interquartile Range (IQR), which represents the spread of the middle 50% of the data.

$$\text{IQR} = Q_{0.75} - Q_{0.25}$$

The IQR is invaluable because, unlike the variance, it remains defined and useful even when the underlying distribution is non-normal or highly skewed.

Quantiles vs. Measures of Central Tendency

Statistic Measure What it Represents Sensitivity to Outliers
Mean ($\bar{x}$) Center The “balance point” (sum/count). High. A single extreme value can pull the mean significantly.
Median ($Q_{0.5}$) Center The middle point (50% split). Low. Only dependent on the relative order of the data, not the magnitude of the extremes.
Interquartile Range (IQR) Spread The spread of the middle 50% of the data. ($Q_{0.75} - Q_{0.25}$) Low. Focuses only on the core, robust data.

The primary utility of quantiles is that they provide a robust understanding of data structure, unaffected by the undue influence of extreme outliers, unlike the mean.

The Behavior of Quantiles Across Distributions

The relationship between the calculated quantile and the theoretical distribution model is the most illuminating aspect of this topic. We examine three distinct types of distributions to see how quantiles behave.

1. The Uniform Distribution

The Uniform distribution assumes that every value within a specified bounded interval $[a, b]$ is equally likely.

  • Key Formula: The quantile $Q_p$ is found through simple linear interpolation: $$Q_p = a + p(b - a)$$

Calculation Example: A random variable $X$ is uniformly distributed between $a=5$ and $b=15$. We want to find the 75th percentile ($Q_{0.75}$).

Step 1: Identify parameters: $a=5$, $b=15$, $p=0.75$.

Step 2: Apply the Quantile Formula:

$$Q_{0.75} = 5 + 0.75(15 - 5)$$

$$Q_{0.75} = 5 + 0.75(10) = 5 + 7.5 = 12.5$$

Conclusion: 75% of the data falls below 12.5.

Summary: Quantiles are perfectly linear and easy to calculate.

2. The Gaussian (Normal) Distribution

The Normal distribution is defined by its symmetry and parameterized by its mean ($\mu$) and standard deviation ($\sigma$).

  • Symmetry: Perfect symmetry around $\mu$.
  • Key Relationship: $\mu = Q_{0.50}$
  • Quantile Behavior (Theoretical): The quantiles are mapped using the standard normal distribution $Z$. To find the quantile $Q_p$, we find the Z-score corresponding to $p$ and then transform it back to the scale of the distribution: $$Q_p = \mu + Z_p \cdot \sigma$$ Where $Z_p$ is the $p$-th quantile of the standard normal distribution (i.e., $\Phi(Z_p) = p$).

Theoretical Example: Assume $\mu=50$ and $\sigma=10$. We want to find the 90th percentile ($Q_{0.90}$).

Step 1: Find the Z-score ($Z_{0.90}$): Using a standard Z-table (or statistical software), we find that the Z-score corresponding to 90% cumulative probability is approximately $1.28$.

Step 2: Apply the Quantile Formula:

$$Q_{0.90} = 50 + 1.28 \cdot 10 = 50 + 12.8 = 62.8$$

Conclusion: 90% of the data falls below 62.8.

Summary: The quantiles are predictable and directly scaled by $\sigma$.

3. The Cauchy Distribution

The Cauchy distribution highlights the unique power of quantiles by failing the conditions required for moments (mean and variance) to exist.

  • Failure of Moments: Both the expected value and variance are undefined. $$\text{Mean: } E[X] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x f(x) dx \quad \text{(Does not converge)}$$
  • Quantile Reliability: The median and IQR are defined and calculable.

Conceptual Example: While we cannot calculate the mean or variance, we can calculate the median and the IQR using the known formula structure derived from the CDF:

Goal: Find the Median ($Q_{0.50}$) and IQR for a Cauchy distribution with location parameter $x_0$ and scale $\gamma$.

Median Formula: For the Cauchy distribution, the median is simply the location parameter $x_0$:

$$Q_{0.50} = x_0$$

IQR Formula (Theoretical): The IQR can be derived from the standard deviation $\gamma$:

$$\text{IQR} = 2 \gamma$$

Significance: The ability to calculate the median and IQR (measures of position and spread) even when the defining parameters (mean and variance) are undefined is the strongest testament to the utility of quantiles.

Quantile Analysis

Quantile analysis is not merely an alternative to mean calculation; it is a fundamentally more powerful and robust approach to describing data.

By providing measures like the median and the Interquartile Range, quantiles allow data scientists to:

  1. Identify skewness and asymmetry immediately.
  2. Determine the true spread of the most central portion of the data.
  3. Maintain stable analysis even when faced with extreme outliers or distributions (like the Cauchy) for which traditional moments fail.

1. Identifying Skewness and Asymmetry

The Theory: A symmetrical distribution is perfectly balanced; the mean, median, and mode coincide. If these three measures are unequal, the data is skewed.

  • Positively Skewed (Right Skew): The Mean $>$ Median. The tail pulls the mean upwards (e.g., income).
  • Negatively Skewed (Left Skew): The Mean $<$ Median. The tail pulls the mean downwards (e.g., exam scores where most people score high).

Why Quantiles are Key: The median ($Q_{0.50}$) acts as the single most reliable measure of the center of mass, unaffected by the skewness created by the long tail. By comparing $\text{Mean} \text{ vs. } \text{Median}$, data scientists can instantly diagnose the shape of the data.

Step-by-Step Example: Analyzing Income Data (Wealth Inequality)

Imagine a small town’s annual income data (in thousands of dollars):

$$D = \{20, 25, 30, 35, 40, 50, 60, 150, 500\}$$

(Note the highly influential salaries of 150 and 500).

Step 1: Calculate the Mean (The Misleading Measure):

$$\text{Mean} = \frac{20 + 25 + 30 + 35 + 40 + 50 + 60 + 150 + 500}{9} = \frac{860}{9} \approx 95.56$$

Interpretation: The average income is nearly $\$96,000$. This suggests that the typical resident earns close to $$96k$.

Step 2: Calculate the Median (The Robust Measure): (The median is the 5th term since $N=9$)

$$\text{Median} = 40$$

Interpretation: The median income is $\$40,000$. This means half the population earns less than $$40k$ and half earns more.

Step 3: Diagnose Skewness:

$$\text{Mean} (95.56) > \text{Median} (40)$$

Conclusion: The large, infrequent incomes (150 and 500) have pulled the mean far to the right, indicating a positive (right) skew. The median provides the accurate, typical value, demonstrating that the mean is misleading in the presence of significant wealth inequality.

2. Determining the True Spread of the Most Central Portion of the Data

The Theory: Measures of spread quantify how far the data points are from the center. The variance ($\sigma^2$) and standard deviation ($\sigma$) use every single data point in their calculation. This makes them highly susceptible to being inflated by extreme outliers, giving a falsely inflated sense of variability.

Why Quantiles are Key: The Interquartile Range ($\text{IQR} = Q_{0.75} - Q_{0.25}$) disregards the top 25% and the bottom 25% of the data. It is solely focused on the spread of the stable, central 50%, giving a true, reliable measure of typical variability.

Step-by-Step Example: Analyzing Employee Performance Scores

Consider a dataset of 10 test scores (out of 100) where one score is a clear outlier:

$$D = \{75, 78, 80, 82, 85, 86, 88, 90, 92, 20\}$$

(The score 20 is a major outlier, possibly a test error.)

Step 1: Calculate Standard Deviation (Using all data):

$$\text{Standard Deviation} (\sigma) \approx 30.0$$

Interpretation: The standard deviation suggests massive variability. A standard deviation of 30 points suggests that the data is extremely spread out.

Step 2: Calculate the IQR (Focusing only on the middle 50%):

  • $Q_{0.25} = 78$
  • $Q_{0.75} = 89$ $$\text{IQR} = 89 - 78 = 11$$ Interpretation: The IQR indicates that the vast majority of the scores (the middle half) are confined within a very narrow range of 11 points.

Comparison: The $\sigma$ of $30$ suggests extreme inconsistency, but the $\text{IQR}$ of $11$ reveals that the core performance of the group is remarkably consistent. The $\text{IQR}$ provides a much more accurate picture of the typical skill level, filtered out the extreme noise generated by the outlier (20).

3. Maintaining Stable Analysis When Moments Fail (The Cauchy Case)

The Theory: This is the ultimate proof of quantile superiority. In some exotic or pathological distributions, calculating the mean ($\mu$) and variance ($\sigma^2$) is mathematically impossible because the mathematical integrals defining these moments diverge.

Why Quantiles are Key: Quantiles are defined through the Cumulative Distribution Function (CDF), which relies on the ordering of data, not the sum or the squares of the data. Since the ordering always exists, the median and quantiles are always computable, even when the classical moments are not.

Conceptual Example: The Extreme Tail Case (Cauchy)

Consider a distribution shaped like the Cauchy curve. Due to its heavy tails, extreme events pull the average away in all directions.

Measure Definition Result for Cauchy Why?
Mean ($\mu$) $E[X]$ Undefined The area under the curve integral fails to converge.
Variance ($\sigma^2$) $E[(X-\mu)^2]$ Undefined The higher moment integral fails to converge.
Median ($Q_{0.50}$) 50th percentile Defined Based only on relative position.
IQR $Q_{0.75} - Q_{0.25}$ Defined Based only on relative position.

Conclusion: In situations like the Cauchy (or other heavy-tailed distributions), the mean and variance give us zero actionable information. However, the quantiles instantly provide stable, interpretable metrics. They offer a dependable framework for summarizing the data even when the underlying physics of the data are unstable.

Practical Applications of Quantile Analysis

Beyond academic theory, understanding quantiles is not just mathematically interesting—it is critical for building robust, real-world models in data science and finance. Quantiles provide the necessary robustness when assumptions about normality fail.

1. Financial Modeling: Value-at-Risk (VaR)

The Problem: Financial assets, particularly stock returns, are notoriously non-normal. They often exhibit “fat tails,” meaning extreme movements (market crashes or sudden booms) happen much more frequently than a Gaussian model would predict. Relying on the mean return or standard deviation to estimate risk is dangerously inaccurate.

The Tool: Value-at-Risk (VaR) uses quantiles to answer a single question: What is the maximum loss we expect over a given time period with a certain level of confidence?

Conceptual Example (99th Percentile Loss): Imagine an investment portfolio. The firm wants to calculate its VaR at the 99% confidence level for one day.

  • Goal: Determine the loss ($L$) such that there is only a 1% chance of the actual loss exceeding $L$.
  • Statistic Used: The 1st percentile ($Q_{0.01}$).

Step-by-Step Process:

  1. Gather Data: The analyst gathers 5 years of daily return data (e.g., $R_1, R_2, \dots, R_{1250}$).
  2. Sort the Data: The daily returns are sorted from worst (most negative) to best (most positive).
  3. Identify the Percentile Index: For a 1% VaR, we are looking for the value at the 1st percentile.
  4. Calculate $Q_{0.01}$: The 1st percentile (the 1st quantile) is the extreme negative return that separates the worst 1% of outcomes from the rest.
    • Suppose the calculation yields: $Q_{0.01} = -5.2\%$.
  5. Interpret the VaR: The calculated value ($Q_{0.01}$) represents the maximum expected loss.
    • Conclusion: The firm reports that its 1-day VaR is 5.2%. This means, “We are 99% confident that we will not lose more than 5.2% of our capital tomorrow.”

Why Quantiles are Superior: Because $Q_{0.01}$ is based on the empirical order of outcomes, it is directly impacted by the sheer severity of the worst observed returns, giving a much more realistic view of potential systemic risk than simple standard deviation calculations.

2. Data Cleaning and Outlier Detection

The Problem: Real-world datasets are often contaminated by errors, measurement flaws, or extreme, non-representative events (outliers). Using simple mean-based rules (e.g., $X > 3\sigma$) to flag outliers assumes the data is normally distributed, which is often false.

The Tool: The Interquartile Range (IQR) method uses quantiles to define boundaries that are immune to non-normal distribution tails.

Step-by-Step Example: Suppose we measure the salaries of 9 employees (in thousands of dollars):

$$D = \{30, 35, 38, 40, 42, 45, 50, 55, 150\}$$

(Note: The salary of 150 is a likely outlier.)

Goal: Identify outliers using the IQR method.

Step 1: Calculate Quartiles:

  • $Q_{0.25}$ (First Quartile): The value separating the bottom 25%.
    • Calculation: Using the formula or standard software methods on $D$, $Q_{0.25} = 38$.
  • $Q_{0.75}$ (Third Quartile): The value separating the top 25%.
    • Calculation: $Q_{0.75} = 45$.

Step 2: Calculate the IQR:

$$\text{IQR} = Q_{0.75} - Q_{0.25} = 45 - 38 = 7$$

Step 3: Establish the Fences (Boundaries): We define “reasonable” bounds using $1.5 \times \text{IQR}$.

  • Lower Bound: $Q_{0.25} - 1.5 \times \text{IQR} = 38 - 1.5(7) = 38 - 10.5 = 27.5$
  • Upper Bound: $Q_{0.75} + 1.5 \times \text{IQR} = 45 + 1.5(7) = 45 + 10.5 = 55.5$

Step 4: Identify Outliers: Any data point outside the $[27.5, 55.5]$ range is considered a potential outlier.

  • Checking the dataset: $\{30, 35, 38, 40, 42, 45, 50, 55, \mathbf{150}\}$.
  • The value $150$ is far above the upper bound of $55.5$.

Conclusion: The IQR method successfully flags 150 as a significant outlier without assuming that the salary distribution should be perfectly symmetrical or normally distributed.

3. Percentile Ranking

The Problem: When comparing scores or performance metrics from different sources (e.g., standardized test scores vs. time trial records), the raw scores use different scales, making direct comparison misleading.

The Tool: Quantile ranking normalizes the data, converting raw scores into relative percentile ranks (a standardized scale from 0 to 1).

Step-by-Step Example: A hiring manager is comparing three candidates for a role. The test scores use different measurement systems:

  • Candidate A (English Score): 85/100
  • Candidate B (Math Score): 0.82 (on a scale of 0 to 1)
  • Candidate C (Critical Thinking): 88 (on a scale of 0 to 100)

Goal: Determine who is the most proficient relative to the group, regardless of the score scale.

Method: We determine the percentile rank of each candidate’s score within the group’s performance range.

  1. Calculate Relative Position: We normalize the scores to a standard 0 to 1 scale.

    • Candidate A: $\text{Rank}_A = \frac{85}{100} = 0.85$
    • Candidate B: $\text{Rank}_B = 0.82$ (Already on scale)
    • Candidate C: $\text{Rank}_C = \frac{88}{100} = 0.88$

  2. Interpret the Ranks: The standardized ranks provide a single, apples-to-apples comparison.

    • Candidate A scored in the 85th percentile.
    • Candidate B scored in the 82nd percentile.
    • Candidate C scored in the 88th percentile.

Conclusion: By using percentile ranking, the manager can definitively say that Candidate C is the most proficient, even though they had the highest raw score. Quantiles transform heterogeneous scores into a homogeneous measure of relative performance.
