Decay Functions: The Power of Forgetting

📅 August 12, 2026 • ⏱️ 5 min read
🏷️ Tags: Functions Decay Time Series

In the world of data science, finance, and predictive modeling, the assumption that the past perfectly predicts the future is often a dangerous oversimplification. Real-world systems are dynamic; recent events carry more weight, or relevance, than events that happened years ago.

This realization has given rise to Decay Functions—mathematical tools designed not merely to average data, but to weight it, acknowledging that data loses significance over time. These functions allow analysts to build sophisticated predictions that are responsive to the present while remaining grounded in history.

Here is a deep dive into the primary types of decay, and how they mathematically adjust the importance of past data.

What is Data Decay?

Data decay, or time decay, is the principle that the predictive power of an observation decreases as the time elapsed since that observation increases.

Instead of treating all historical data points as equally important (the Simple Moving Average), decay functions introduce a weight ($\omega$) to each data point ($D_t$).

$$\text{Weighted Average} = \frac{\sum_{t=1}^{N} (\text{Data Point}_t \times \text{Weight}_t)}{\sum_{t=1}^{N} \text{Weight}_t}$$

1. The Baseline: Simple Moving Average (SMA)

The simplest form of averaging assumes no decay at all. Every point contributes equally.

  • Concept: Equal importance.
  • Formula Example: $(6 + 2 + 8 + 5 + 9) / 5 = 30 / 5 = 6$.

In this example, the data points (6, 2, 8, 5, 9) are equally weighted. The SMA is simple, effective for stable data, but fatally flawed when rapid shifts or recent changes are the most important signal.

The Decay Models

When a Simple Moving Average is insufficient, we introduce a weighting scheme based on how rapidly we want the data’s influence to fade.

2. Linear Decay (Arithmetic Decay)

Linear decay assumes that the weight of the data decreases at a constant, steady rate. The decline in importance is linear, often modeled by a simple counter or an arithmetic progression.

  • Concept: Predictable, constant loss of relevance.
  • Weighting Pattern: $1, 2, 3, 4, 5, \dots$ (or $N, N-1, N-2, \dots$).

In this model, the newest data point usually receives the highest weight, and the oldest data point receives the lowest.

The Mathematics of Linear Decay

If we have five data points, and we assign weights that increase linearly (where 1 is the oldest and 5 is the newest):

$$\text{Weighted Average} = \frac{(6 \cdot 1) + (2 \cdot 2) + (8 \cdot 3) + (5 \cdot 4) + (9 \cdot 5)}{(1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5)}$$

Interpretation: The newest value (9) is multiplied by 5, giving it the greatest influence. The oldest value (6) is multiplied by 1, giving it the least influence.

  • Pros: Simple to implement and understand.
  • Cons: It assumes that the rate of forgetting is constant, which is rarely true in complex systems.

3. Exponential Decay (Geometric Decay)

Exponential decay is the most common and theoretically robust method for modeling forgetting. It assumes that the weight decreases multiplicatively (by a fixed factor) over time.

  • Concept: The loss of relevance is proportional to the current weight; the further back in time, the faster the influence shrinks.
  • Weighting Pattern: $1, r, r^2, r^3, \dots$ (where $r$ is the decay rate, typically $r < 1$).

The power of exponential decay lies in its ability to model genuine “memory fades”—a recent event is significantly more important than an event from a short while ago, which is far more important than an event from years ago.

The Mathematics of Exponential Decay

Let’s revisit the five data points, but this time, we use powers of 2 (a rapid decline) as our weights, where the most recent data point is weighted by $2^2$ (or similar escalating powers, depending on how you define the timeline).

If we use the weights $1, 2^2, 3^2$ (using squares for escalating importance):

$$\text{Weighted Average} = \frac{(6 \cdot 1) + (2 \cdot 2^2) + (8 \cdot 3^3) + \dots}{1 + 2^2 + 3^3 + \dots}$$

Interpretation:

  • The first point (6) is multiplied by $1$.
  • The second point (2) is multiplied by $2^2 = 4$.
  • The third point (8) is multiplied by $3^3 = 27$.

Notice how the weights ($1, 4, 27$) accelerate, assigning increasingly dramatic weight to the most recent observations.

  • Pros: Highly realistic; models true decay processes (e.g., forgetting, memory retention, decaying financial momentum).
  • Cons: Determining the correct decay rate ($\lambda$ or $r$) requires deep domain knowledge and significant calibration.

Comparison Table

Feature Simple Moving Average (SMA) Linear Decay Exponential Decay
Weighting Rule All weights = 1 Weights increase arithmetically (e.g., $1, 2, 3, 4$) Weights increase geometrically (e.g., $1, r^2, r^3$)
Assumed Decay None Constant rate of forgetting Rate of forgetting accelerates over time
Impact on Data Smooth, uniform response Predictable response Quick, sharp responsiveness to current changes
Best Used When Data is stationary and highly predictable (e.g., steady average temperature). Decay is known to be linear and predictable. The signal is critical and recent data dominates the outcome (e.g., stock trading, reaction time analysis).

Conclusion: Choosing Your Weight

The choice between these methods is not merely mathematical—it is fundamentally philosophical. It is a choice about how you believe reality forgets.

  • If you believe history is equally important: Use SMA.
  • If you believe importance fades steadily and predictably: Use Linear Decay.
  • If you believe importance fades rapidly and accelerates, giving near-total authority to the present: Use Exponential Decay.

By accurately modeling the decay function, data scientists move beyond simple averages and build models that truly reflect the dynamic, forgetful, and ever-changing nature of the real world.
