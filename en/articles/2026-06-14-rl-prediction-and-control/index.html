RL Prediction and Control

📅 June 14, 2026 • ⏱️ 5 min read
🏷️ Tags: Reinforcement Learning

In the field of Reinforcement Learning (RL), every algorithm, every dataset, and every research paper aims to solve a problem. While many algorithms seem to do the same thing — learn what is “good” — they are fundamentally attempting to answer two distinct, yet related, questions:

  1. Prediction: “If I follow this specific set of rules, what will the average outcome be?”
  2. Control: “What should my set of rules be, so that the outcome will be the absolute best possible?”

These are the two primary objectives of RL. They represent the difference between assessment and optimization, and understanding this distinction is key to mastering the field.

Prediction: The Art of Assessment (The Value Game)

Prediction is the act of calculating expected value. When an agent focuses on prediction, its goal is descriptive: it wants to know how good a situation is under a given set of circumstances.

The Goal: The Value Function ($V^{\pi}$)

A predictive agent aims to calculate the Value Function. This function assigns a single number to every state (or state-action pair) that represents the expected accumulated future reward, assuming the agent continues to follow a specific policy $\pi$.

  • What it outputs: A number (e.g., “In this state, following the current policy, I expect to earn 15 points”).
  • What it doesn’t output: A set of instructions (it won’t tell you what to do next).
  • Focus: The agent is passive. It accepts the policy ($\pi$) as a given and merely measures its performance.

The Core Mechanism: The Bellman Expectation Equation

Prediction is mathematically governed by the Bellman Expectation Equation. This equation states that the value of a state is the immediate reward plus the expected value of the next state. It calculates an average based on the known policy.

$$\text{Value}(s) = \text{Reward}(s) + \gamma \times E[\text{Value}(s') \mid s, \pi]$$

Here,

  • $\gamma$ is the discount factor, and
  • $E[\dots]$ represents the expectation based on the policy $\pi$.

The Role of MC in Prediction

Monte Carlo methods are perfect for prediction because they are naturally designed to estimate averages. By simulating the full episode many times, we average out the randomness and get a reliable estimate of the true expected return for the given policy.

Control: The Science of Optimization (The Policy Game)

Control is the act of optimization. When an agent focuses on control, its goal is prescriptive: it doesn’t care what is currently happening; it only cares how to change its rules to make the best possible thing happen.

The Goal: The Optimal Policy ($\pi^*$)

A control agent aims to find the Optimal Policy ($\pi^*$). This policy is the set of actions that guarantees the highest possible cumulative reward in the long run. The agent seeks the best rules, not just the rules that currently work.

  • What it outputs: A map or a set of rules (“If I am in this state, I must take this specific action”).
  • What it doesn’t output: A value number (it doesn’t just state a value; it tells you how to achieve that value).
  • Focus: The agent is active. It uses the value assessment to drive its own improvement.

The Core Mechanism: The Bellman Optimality Equation

Control is mathematically governed by the Bellman Optimality Equation. This equation differs from prediction because it incorporates an $\text{max}$ operator. It doesn’t just calculate the expected value; it finds the action $a$ that maximizes the next expected value.

$$\text{Optimal Value}(s) = \text{Reward}(s) + \gamma \times \max_{a} E[\text{Value}(s') \mid s, a]$$

This is the crucial difference: the “max” tells the agent, “Don’t just calculate the average; choose the action that yields the highest possible average!”

The Relationship: How Prediction Fuels Control

It is critical to view prediction and control not as rivals, but as a two-step process:

  1. Prediction First: To know what the best policy ($\pi^*$) is, the agent must first estimate the value of a potential path or state (Prediction).
  2. Control Second: The agent then uses this value information to iteratively improve the policy ($\pi \rightarrow \pi'$), choosing the action that leads to the highest estimated value.
  • The Process: Prediction provides the necessary knowledge (the Value Function). Control is the action (the Policy) that uses that knowledge to maximize the return.

The Analogy: The Student and the Test Score

Imagine a student (the Agent) trying to get the best possible grade (Optimal Policy $\pi^*$) on a massive, confusing exam.

  • Prediction is the Study Session: The student spends hours reviewing material. They practice solving problems and calculate, “If I write the answers this way, I predict I will get 80%.” (They are assessing the potential value.)
  • Control is the Exam Day Strategy: On the day of the exam, the student doesn’t just rely on their prediction; they use their knowledge to actively choose the optimal strategy: “Since I know I must maximize my score, I must skip this section (low value) and spend all my time on the calculus problem (high value).” (They are optimizing their actions based on their assessment.)

The study session (Prediction) is necessary, but the active, strategic selection of answers during the test (Control) is the ultimate goal.

Conclusion

The distinction between prediction and control defines the entire scope of RL research.

  • If the goal is Prediction, we build powerful Value Functions ($V$).
  • If the goal is Control, we build the Optimal Policy ($\pi^*$).

By mastering both the art of accurate assessment (Prediction) and the science of strategic optimization (Control), RL agents can move beyond simply reporting what is, to actively determining what should be.
