Us | Chapter 010 The Evolution of Civilization

Human civilization is cyclical; it persists and flourishes. It follows a pattern of repeating cycles: restarting, flourishing, collapsing, and achieving leapfrog leaps. With every iteration, human society undergoes systemic reshaping: the transfer of power, the reallocation of resources, technological leaps, the reconstruction of culture, and the rebuilding of the spirit.

In the long Paleolithic era, humans were loosely affiliated hunting-gathering collectives, co-existing with nature and animals, where survival was the primary challenge. In the Neolithic period, humans learned to domesticate animals and plant crops, transitioning from nomadic life to settled existence. Settlement was a qualitative leap that externalized the knowledge accumulated by humanity into land, tools, and dwellings. Material abundance transitioned humans from mere survival toward stable reproduction. The increase in population density and material surplus brought about new demands for management.

The earliest centers of civilization largely emerged on the fertile alluvial plains of rivers. The Mesopotamian civilization of the two rivers, the ancient Egyptian civilization of the Nile, the ancient Indian civilization of the Indus River, and the Chinese civilization of the Yangtze and Yellow Rivers—in different geographical locations, within a single historical period—built magnificent structures, invented writing, formulated laws, established central organizational systems, and developed a mainstream value system. These early civilizations laid the foundation for the “meta-system” of civilization: the ruling class, the legal system, the value structure, and the massive engineering projects.

However, different civilizations sprouted independently. The two-river civilizations were located in a conflict zone, a crossroads of multiple ethnicities, and their water resources were subject to periodic changes. Law became the guarantor of order in this volatile and chaotic environment. The Nile River was situated in a basin with predictable, cyclical water resources. This “divinely granted” stability, combined with a divine-right structure, formed the basis of the order there. The Indus Valley had complex and easily defended, yet difficult-to-govern terrain, which led them to transform complex external governance into internal spiritual liberation, making faith the method for maintaining order. The Yangtze and Yellow River regions, located inland, were vast and densely populated, leading to the development of a centralized bureaucratic system as a means of governing huge territories. They used ethics as the social behavioral norm, creating a combination of rule by man (rénzhì) and moral law.

The confluence of the two-river and Mediterranean civilizations began through trade, but conflict accelerated the process. The central authority and massive resource reserves of the two-river civilizations, combined with military oppression, sparked resistance from Mediterranean states. Ancient Greece, aware of its military limitations, focused its energy on the field least conquerable by force—thought. Greece pioneered the philosophy of reason, liberating humanity from the original divine-right narrative. Rational thinking models proposed by Plato and Aristotle systematically questioned established authority for the first time, exploring human essence, the ideal organization of society, and the disciplining of knowledge (logic and science). Greece also absorbed the astronomy, medicine, and mystery traditions of the two-river civilizations, reshaping them through its own highly developed rationality and philosophy, thus forming the Hellenistic era. Meanwhile, ancient Rome absorbed the law and systems of the two rivers, elevating them into universal laws applicable to all humankind. Through law, engineering, and military organization, these laws were spread over a certain geographical scale. The establishment of Roman law became the legal skeleton of Western civilization.

After the collapse of classical civilizations, the Islamic civilization, situated at the junction of the Mediterranean and the Middle East, absorbed Greek philosophy and combined it with Indian and Persian scientific knowledge, initiating the Golden Age and becoming the global center of knowledge. During this time, its mathematics, medicine, and astronomy reached their peaks. Meanwhile, Europe was in a cycle of Christianization and feudalism, and the core value of Western civilization—the awakening of individual spirit and the systematization of faith—was further reinforced. Exchange and inheritance became the core of this period, and knowledge was no longer locally confined; instead, it flowed, blended, and reactivated globally through trade routes (like the Silk Road) and faiths (such as Buddhism, Islam, and Christianity).

When history reached 18th-century Europe, the Enlightenment rejected the traditional academic explanation of everything by divine theology, elevating questioning, the scientific method, and individual rights. In France, scientific progress finally found true social momentum. Montesquieu proposed the “separation of powers,” and Rousseau proposed “natural rights” and the “social contract.” At this point, philosophical speculation shifted from “Why do we exist?” to “How should we organize society?” Netherlands, as a center for early trade and knowledge exchange, became the springboard for these ideas. Britain’s empiricism and the gradually rising wealth of the bourgeoisie provided the practical economic foundation. The dialogues between French and English thinkers matured the Enlightenment. However, the first successful practical implementation of Enlightenment ideas occurred in America. Out of demands for colonial self-rule and liberation from the constraints of the colonial power (Britain), America drafted the Declaration of Independence and the Constitution, embedding concepts like “natural rights,” “the right to life,” and “property rights” into the legal documents of the first modern democratic nation, thus establishing the basic model of a liberal state.

By the late 18th and 19th centuries, scientific progress found a powerful material carrier in the Industrial Revolution. The combination of steam engines, mechanized production, and scientific organizational theory caused human productivity to grow exponentially. The Industrial Revolution first achieved unprecedented efficiency in resource allocation, but it also brought intense conflicts between workers and capitalists, as well as environmental pressure. New problems, such as wealth gaps, the status of the working class, and urban management, arose, elevating early Enlightenment discussion from solving feudalism and divine authority to discussing social science and social justice. By the mid-19th century, figures like Owen and Auguste Comte proposed abolishing laissez-faire capitalism and establishing a collective social structure with greater moral constraints and guarantees. In the latter half of the 19th century, economics, sociology, and demography formally upgraded from philosophical speculation to actionable empirical sciences. Adam Smith and David Ricardo proposed the operating mechanism of the free market, providing theoretical guidance for capitalism. Meanwhile, Émile Durkheim and Max Weber began systematically studying how society functioned, and what structural elements were critical for maintaining social stability.

Towards the end of the 19th and beginning of the 20th century, the accumulation of social wealth reached its peak, driven by the electricity revolution. However, the operating logic of capitalism—the pursuit of infinite growth and absolute free markets—led to massive wealth gaps and the extreme nature of nationalism. World War I (1914-1918), the conflict between the German-Austrian-Hungarian empires and the Anglo-French powers, saw the collapse of the old power structure. The harsh terms of the Treaty of Versailles, especially the punishment imposed on Germany, sowed the seeds for extreme totalitarianism. Simultaneously, Marxist theory matured in this period, combining economics, sociology, and philosophy to propose a systemic revolutionary theory—the inevitability of class struggle. Furthermore, with the accumulation of colonial and cross-cultural exchange, scholars realized that the underlying logic of every civilization was different, and that no single institutional or philosophical model could comprehensively explain all human behavior. Anthropology thus ascended from a peripheral discipline to an important mainstream science.

World War II (1939-1945) saw the Nazi and Fascist movements challenge liberal democracy, but the post-war European imperial and colonial system was completely destroyed. Concurrently, the rise of the United States and the establishment of the United Nations fixed a new global order, making international cooperation and human rights central to the era. At the same time, the traditional laissez-faire capitalism was proven incapable of solving the social problems caused by war and poverty. This led to Keynesian theory guiding the post-war national governance model: accepting the operational mechanism of capitalism, but advocating that the state must intervene through fiscal policy to smooth out cyclical economic crises and guarantee minimum social welfare (such as healthcare, pensions, and education).

Since the latter half of the 20th century, the arrival of the information revolution made bits the material foundation of civilization. The internet, global communication networks, and artificial intelligence allowed the transmission of knowledge, capital, and information at the speed of light. Human connectivity reached an unprecedented level. In this evolution of civilization, the deepest challenge is no longer material scarcity, but information overload and structural imbalance. In an infinite stream of information, human cognition and choice mechanisms are converted into economic resources by capital and power. Algorithms and platforms become the gatekeepers that determine the flow of information and the cognitive landscape. The few tech giants and capitalists who master critical data, core algorithms, and global infrastructure have reshaped a new digital power structure, not only controlling the direction of information flow but also embedding social consensus, economic activity, and even individual identity into a set of rules set by a small elite. Knowledge acquisition and wealth accumulation no longer rely merely on individual effort and talent, but on the access rights and algorithmic filtering that participate in these digital ecosystems.

In the 21st century, General Artificial Intelligence (AGI) is no longer a concept but a systemic, observable reality. It is not a single technical breakthrough, but a comprehensive restructuring of the cognitive ecosystem. The proximity of AGI marks the first time that the definition of human intelligence is transferred. Human cognitive patterns enter a phase of externalized computation—processes like pattern matching and decision reasoning, traditionally considered uniquely human, are efficiently and perfectly outsourced to neural networks. The cost of knowledge acquisition approaches zero, forcing the center of human value to shift from the storage of knowledge to the art of posing hypotheses and the depth of questioning. Humanity begins to question the value of its own existence, but simultaneously learns to coexist with supra-human intelligence. As intelligence entities permeate all areas of human society, the entire social structure forms a new virtual intelligence network. Human behavior, transactions, information flow, and even emotion are captured, categorized, optimized, and utilized by this virtual network. Yet, this is not true AGI, but in this period, humanity is already facing many pressing issues that need to be solved.
