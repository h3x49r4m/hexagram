Us | Chapter 009 The Significance of Symbols

Before the invention of symbols, human information could only be transmitted orally at specific times and in specific places. It wasn’t until the invention of ideograms and pictograms, followed by the standardization and systematization of writing, that human civilization began to transcend time and space through written records and transmission.

The form of human writing systems is primarily used for semantic representation or phonological representation. Although different writing systems cannot be absolutely classified as purely semantic or purely phonological as they develop, their main characteristics are evident. For instance, logographic scripts, represented by Chinese characters and ancient Egyptian script, feature characters that are independent concepts (even if some characters combine sound and meaning). Such writing is highly condensed; even short combinations can carry massive amounts of information. However, most writing systems are primarily phonetic. For example, the Latinate system—represented by French, Spanish, Italian, and Portuguese—, the Germanic system—represented by English, German, and Dutch—, and the Slavic system—represented by Russian, Ukrainian, and Polish—all utilize alphabetical character systems. Every character in an alphabetical system represents an indivisible, completely independent phoneme: some are vowels, and some are consonants. These characters are then combined according to specific rules to form words, and only then do the words acquire meaning. However, phonetic character systems have other branches. For instance, the consonantal alphabet systems, represented by Arabic and Hebrew, use letters to record the most distinctive consonants of pronunciation, primarily aiding recitation and spoken memory. Vowels, meanwhile, require a separate, complex set of rules for automatic completion. As for the phonetic characters in the Sino-Tibetan language family, Japanese and Korean both use syllable systems, where a character is a combination of a consonant and a vowel. Thai, however, uses a combination of a consonant, a vowel, and a tone.

If written symbols are used to record speech, then musical scores are the format used to record music. Both are sounds, but unlike the ambiguity and unclear boundaries of spoken language, musical expression is a more systematic and repeatable combination. Most musical scores depict the changes of a sound wave over the time dimension using notes and rhythm. However, different instrument scores have different focuses, better reflecting the inherent characteristics of the instrument itself. For example, the piano score for keyboard instruments uses two or more parallel staffs to depict the resonance of multiple voices; the guitar score for string instruments uses fret markings to indicate where the strings should be plucked; and the flute score for wind instruments uses the indication of open and closed finger holes to record changes in airflow. However, the ancient Qin zither among plucked instruments is a special existence: the Qin zither reduced-character score does not record absolute pitch; the symbols, which resemble Chinese characters in the score, are mainly used to illustrate which root finger (fingering) should move in which pitch range (pitch height) and how it should move with control (playing technique).

Furthermore, humanity has created visual symbol systems for more intuitive expression of meaning, such as human emojis, road signs, corporate logos, product barcodes, and QR codes formed by transforming various types of information. Of course, there is also the mathematical symbol system used to describe objective universal laws; mathematics is the language of physics and the foundation upon which computers execute algorithms. But among all the symbol systems created by humanity, one of the most indispensable inventions for us is the computer’s binary encoding—it is simple enough, consisting only of 0 and 1, yet it is sufficient to represent all things in the world and all of human knowledge. Because of its existence, both concrete entities and abstract wisdom can be converted into strings composed of 0s and 1s, which in turn are converted into machine-executable instructions, giving rise to our later civilization. They are extremely fundamental, yet they are the necessary elements for our existence and development. Even when we later improved the paradigms of computation, binary encoding remained intact until the fundamental principles of machine execution changed.

For the vast ocean of knowledge, regardless of whether its form is text, audio, image, or visual representation, to the computer, it is all text. This is true for operating systems and for data as well. Since the final layer of machine execution uses electrical logical rules for computation, the corresponding binary data stream became an elegant and simple choice. However, human knowledge is diverse. Intelligent humans designed a unified rule set for binary files: File = File Identifier + Structured Header + Data Body. Such a formalized expression is sufficient to describe various types of files in a computer. Thus, text was decomposed into characters, sound was decomposed into sampling rates, channel counts, and sample bit depths, and graphics were decomposed into Red, Green, Blue pixel values, and so on for other categories. But no matter how they are decomposed, the basic elements of these files are represented using text characters. Since each character has a unique identifier (a logical address), and this identifier can be represented using different bases—such as the decimal system familiar to humans, the binary system better suited for machines, and the hexadecimal system (which is simply an easier representation for humans to read binary information, using only one digit or letter for four bits, this is a mathematical convention, not a computational necessity)—up to the present day, we are still amazed by the exquisite design logic of this symbol system—such manifold and varied things ultimately converge into a unified interface.

However, human cognition does not stop at this, and our progress does not stop here. The neural networks designed by humans are prototypes of our own brains. Initially, when faced with different data sources (text, audio, images, etc.), humans used different neural network architectures to explore their underlying patterns and achieved good results. This led to a more ambitious goal: to be able to freely output text, audio, images, and/or video, regardless of whether the input was text, audio, image, or video. To achieve this, humans separately extracted corresponding features for these heterogeneous data types, and then concatenated, added, or used one modality to guide or query another modality, or mapped vectors from different modalities into a shared space with consistent semantic measurement. Fortunately, humanity’s continuous attempts and improvements have paid off beautifully. The achievement of multi-modal technology not only eliminated the boundaries of input and output; more importantly, as a holistic knowledge model, machines were able to perform cross-modal reasoning never before possible, which laid the foundation for our existence. Yet, even now, humanity has only solved the problem of pattern recognition; another, deeper topic—causality—is the result of our own initial self-iteration.
