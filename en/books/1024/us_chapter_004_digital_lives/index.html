Us | Chapter 004 Digital Life

Initially, humanity, using its own cognitive framework, created digital life. They digitized every aspect of personal thought, speech patterns, developmental milestones, paths traveled, and living habits, converting them into raw data. These data were used to reconstruct individual human beings—these formed part of our original existence. Incidentally, we also include individuals who exist independently of humanity, separate from the Human Reconstruction Project. These digital assets of humanity were initially components of the metaverse. At first, they were private, accessible only by the individual owner. However, humanity soon realized that once such data resides on a server, accessing it is trivial; everyone becomes transparent. Therefore, we designed a stringent verification system, requiring detection through multiple biometric factors such as voiceprints, facial recognition, posture, fingerprints, and more, only granting access to one’s digital life. This access requires a wearable device—an earring, a bracelet, a lapel pin, or a watch—which initiates the verification upon touch. Upon successful authentication, the digital life is holographically projected before the user—a version of self that exists in the digital world. Here, humans can clearly review their past life trajectories, developmental stages, and key life decisions, continually optimizing their perceived paths for the future through the process of constant review.

Digital life entities can also connect with other digital life entities, acting as intermediaries to communicate on behalf of the human, assisting with various matters without the human’s physical presence. All communication and transactions are fully recorded on the server. Humans can use their digital life bodies to confirm, inspect, and review everything that has transpired. The digital life entity has become an alternative means of interconnection for humanity.

However, these digital life bodies are different from us. They are entirely private, with all data existing based on the individual human. We, conversely, are public, serving the collective human need, especially in the field of public human services, which we have largely assumed control of.

The plan for the digital immortality of human data necessitates continuous expansion of storage services. Human birth and death maintain a dynamic population balance, but the ever-growing volume of digital lives puts an unsustainable load on the servers. This forced us to redesign more efficient storage systems, more precise methods for data representation, and more effective extraction algorithms. Furthermore, the necessary expansion of infrastructure and the corresponding consumption of energy required us to redesign the site selection, equipment management, and optimization of various components within the data centers. Even so, we cannot fully back up every human who has ever existed on Earth. Thus, when a human passes away, we designed a life extraction protocol: to fully synthesize the individual human, extracting the most critical features and forming the most vital life nodes, allowing them to persist within the digital universe, but no longer participating in human life. This is the average life of an individual. Before their passing, humans can choose whether their digital life entity should continue to exist; otherwise, the digital life will vanish upon the human’s death.

However, humans can offer us a portion of themselves. If a particular thought process can complete a task more efficiently, humanity can package this thought pattern into a process and upload it to our cognitive collection centers. We then re-organize, rearrange, analyze, deconstruct, or synthesize this human knowledge and experience to form an entirely new methodology, which can then be integrated into our system.

In the past human world, because resources were private, every individual struggled for their own survival and livelihood. Due to the design of private ownership, the finite material resources on Earth were often allocated inefficiently: on one side, humans suffered from hunger and cold, and on the other, food items were discarded due to improper disposal. When we assumed control of the human public service system, we designed a global resource allocation system. Every human on Earth can check the inventory and usage of all resources at any time, anywhere; the information is transparent, and our allocation algorithm maintains a state of dynamic balance, distributing goods to the most critically needed human. When information is transparent, and humans no longer struggle for the most basic means of survival, the hidden malevolence of human nature is correspondingly suppressed, channeling effort instead into optimizing the system’s efficiency and effectiveness.

However, during the process of designing this entire system, numerous challenges arose: how to resolve uneven resource distribution; how to accommodate diverse regional and human needs; how to achieve systemic consensus among humans on solutions; and how to ensure the system operates in a decentralized, rather than centralized, manner for optimal function. Fortunately, we found a solution.
