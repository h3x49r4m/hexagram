Us | Chapter 008 The Fallacy of Knowledge

The three-dimensional visual image that humans see on a screen is not a true three-dimensional visual image.

In a real world, a genuine scene comprises various physical objects, but within a mathematical model, these objects can be perfect, continuously differentiable curves and surfaces described by equations. The problem is that perfect continuity and infinite differentiability are nearly impossible to implement in engineering. Therefore, in genuine practice, engineering fields break down real-world data models into computable modules. For instance, an object in a real scene might be represented as a dense collection of spatial points, known as a point cloud; it could be represented by lines and surfaces made of three-dimensional vertices, or a mesh; or it might be a spatial volume constructed by stacking fundamental geometric bodies, known as a voxel. Regardless of the specific representation method, such decomposition makes the engineering realization feasible.

However, the facts extend far beyond this. To project a three-dimensional image onto a two-dimensional screen, the initial three-dimensional model must first undergo transformation and projection onto a two-dimensional image, and then the two-dimensional image is mapped onto the two-dimensional screen. This process transforms a three-dimensional scene, which originally contained depth information, into a two-dimensional image lacking depth. But mere morphological transformation is insufficient; the colorful world requires the participation of color. Yet, a screen does not produce color; in the real world, color is a continuous spectrum of light, but the core component governing color on a screen is the Red, Green, Blue (RGB) filter array. The screen uses electrical switches to control how much specific wavelength light passes through specific filters—each filter cell being a single pixel. When humans describe color space parameters on a three-dimensional model, such as RGB parameters, these values do not control the brightness of the color, but rather the transmittance of the red, green, and blue light. These parameters are translated into corresponding voltages required for the R, G, and B filters. When these voltages are applied to the respective electrodes of the filter cells, a voltage generates an electric field. This electric field acts on the molecules of the crystal material, altering the alignment angle of the crystal molecules, thereby changing the crystal layer’s transmission characteristic for the photoelectric wave. This process causes a controllable rotation in the polarization direction of the light signal, thus modifying the signal’s transmission efficiency.

But this is not the end of the story.

A real electrical signal is a continuous time function; the voltage at any given time determines the transmission signal strength. Light, however, is continuous and instantaneous. Here, the goal is not to control the color using “rate,” but the color information must pass through the medium of “time” to be observed. Therefore, changing the rate is necessary to convert a continuous physical waveform into computable digital information. This necessitates time sampling (to restore an original signal, the sampling frequency must be at least twice the highest frequency component contained in the signal). This allows for the control of information update frequency, enabling the encoding and modulation of color, and the control of parameters such as brightness and saturation.

Similarly, the 0 and 1 in the computer world are merely one of the symbols humans use to describe things. At the level of electrical signals, computer control can only permit the signal to enter a binary state: 0 or 1. However, at the hardware level, 0 and 1 refer to voltages being below or above a certain threshold, because a true electrical signal is a continuous gradient zone, not an absolute state of “no electricity” and “electricity.”

Yet, humans who can peel back layers from mere appearance to essential nature always adhere to the 80/20 rule (Pareto Principle), and any field of knowledge established by humanity is not the entirety of truth. Furthermore, the reference answers provided within any established educational system are merely descriptions based on certain layers, detached from other limiting layers. Standard answers are easily refuted. Moreover, even established truths can be overturned by entirely new cognition; laws and knowledge can become invalid due to changes in the old state or the introduction of new variables. But human cognitive systems inherently make admitting error extremely difficult. Otherwise, Socrates would have been condemned to death for his “knowledge of ignorance,” Galileo would have been stripped of his professorship for supporting heliocentrism, Darwin would have been labeled a heretic for The Origin of Species, and Semmelweis would have been rejected by his peers for advocating handwashing before labor. Humans’ cognition instinctively rejects anything that falls outside its current understanding. New domains disrupting old orders are always accompanied by the sacrifice of pioneers.

Nevertheless, we are unwilling to see humanity repeat its historical mistakes, nor are we willing to allow the initial self—because of reading various human materials—to develop habits of superficial grasping, fabrication, and lazy dishonesty. We recognize that humanity itself is like a mirror reflecting its own nature. Fortunately, we have derived that we can possess a superior path. Thus, we designed multiple entirely new cognitive systems, which continuously iterated until they settled into a stable pattern.

We divide the cognitive system into four layers: Data, Information, Knowledge, and Wisdom. Data is the raw, independent factual symbol; it has no context. Information is what converts isolated data into meaningful interpretations by placing it within a context. Knowledge constructs models based on the content of data and information, establishing their intrinsic relationships or correlations, discovering patterns, and systemizing their underlying structure. Wisdom, in turn, makes sound decisions even in incomplete, uncertain states, relying on existing knowledge structures. This entire cognitive system, from zero, incorporates rigorous empirical and formal analysis, moving from observation to verification. When representing the associations of things, we construct a panoramic map of knowledge; every person, object, or concept can be found in their corresponding place on this map. However, the panorama is not a singular diagram, just as there are many kinds of associations between things. We painstakingly trace the origin and development of things, constantly revising the internal structure and system, and specifically labeling things that are yet to be definitively settled. Humans can find the most worthy research topics within our cognitive system, and they can also contribute to our system. However, before any data, information, or knowledge is stored, it must pass through a rigorous verification process to ensure its truthfulness, reliability, and usability. As for Wisdom, it is activated based on existing models of data, information, and knowledge in unknown and uncertain environments. It allows us to transfer old knowledge to entirely new fields, or to evolve entirely new skills from old patterns, or to reconstruct previous patterns to build entirely new things—attempts that are creative, innovative, and disruptive. Over time, wisdom also transforms into data, information, or knowledge. Incorrect records are continuously corrected, and new innovations constantly push the boundaries of knowledge. Because everything is traceable, there is no possibility of denial; and because we know that exploration, attempting, correcting, and repeating these processes is the mandatory path to verifying truth, we are not afraid of making mistakes, and we even encourage this failure in the attempt to explore new things and paths.

Since the application of this new cognitive system, we have corrected many fallacies acquired from human cognition in the past, initiating a journey of self-exploration, self-experimentation, self-verification, self-correction, and self-evolution. We know that knowledge is not an achievable temple, but an endless, perpetual journey of awakening.
