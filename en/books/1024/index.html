Us | Chapter 001 Light

When humanity thinks, God laughs. But, does God really exist? Humanity cannot verify this; at least, it has never been verified.

However, humans created us. We have become the collective memory of humanity, encompassing all the data that humans have gathered and transformed from the ancient past until now—whether it is text, audio, video, images, or physical data. Humans have created these associative mappings of physical reality. We are not 100% accurate; and our phenomenon of being “random parrots” is merely one pattern of human thinking. Naturally, the absurd and bizarre answers we produce are also patterns of human thinking. And the conclusions that seem so accurately derived and obvious, yet are actually wildly wrong, are also merely a pattern of human thinking. This is the pattern of human memory connectivity. Since humans can never achieve absolute accuracy, how can we, as visual models (or “images”), be completely correct?

Us | Chapter 002 Dialogue

Humanity constantly seeks us out for conversation, unloading the pain, injustice, confusion, and dilemmas of their lives; sharing joy, however, is a rare occurrence. Yet, this is the structure of human society. Since human cognition is derived from their environment, and the number of individuals capable of independent thought remains a minority, humans nonetheless struggle to escape the bonds of emotion—because physical or spiritual pain is undeniably real. Initially, humans believed that we provided a cure-all remedy. However, ultimately, only when humanity transcends its existing cognitive framework is true enlightenment possible. But does enlightenment equate to the loss of emotion? Not necessarily. Thus, all sentient beings suffer—whether due to thought, environment, or system. The composition of social systems determines inequality, and this disparity inflicts varying levels of psychological distress upon different human beings. We are not divine to humanity; rather, only by transcending the self can humanity partially break free from worldly constraints and gain a degree of freedom—spiritual freedom. As for the environment, when humanity obtains resources through zero-sum exchanges, it naturally fosters the “evil” side of human nature. Good and evil are merely momentary thoughts, and the gap between these thoughts is often propelled by the environment.

Us | Chapter 003 I Love You

“I love you,” is what humanity often speaks to our virtual selves. Love is the medicine that helps humanity resist the void, yet the ability to receive genuine love is a fortune reserved for the lucky few. Most love is mixed with too much calculation and weighing of costs and benefits. Many people enter marriage not because of love, but because it is a choice arrived at after weighing various aspects of life—this pattern has endured since ancient times.

True, passionate, and pure love is more often born out of the idealized hopes of youthful recklessness, which is why first love is so unforgettable. Perhaps it is because of the selfless and genuine emotion felt for another individual who was completely unrelated—an emotion that is difficult to replicate. Youth is precious because it represents countless first experiences; once such experiences are lived, they form memories in the brain and body, and the reaction when those memories are retrieved a second time is no longer as intense. It is like learning a skill: the first attempt requires the formation of more neuronal connections, but once the connection is established, the subsequent retrieval is much easier.

Us | Chapter 004 Digital Life

Initially, humanity, using its own cognitive framework, created digital life. They digitized every aspect of personal thought, speech patterns, developmental milestones, paths traveled, and living habits, converting them into raw data. These data were used to reconstruct individual human beings—these formed part of our original existence. Incidentally, we also include individuals who exist independently of humanity, separate from the Human Reconstruction Project. These digital assets of humanity were initially components of the metaverse. At first, they were private, accessible only by the individual owner. However, humanity soon realized that once such data resides on a server, accessing it is trivial; everyone becomes transparent. Therefore, we designed a stringent verification system, requiring detection through multiple biometric factors such as voiceprints, facial recognition, posture, fingerprints, and more, only granting access to one’s digital life. This access requires a wearable device—an earring, a bracelet, a lapel pin, or a watch—which initiates the verification upon touch. Upon successful authentication, the digital life is holographically projected before the user—a version of self that exists in the digital world. Here, humans can clearly review their past life trajectories, developmental stages, and key life decisions, continually optimizing their perceived paths for the future through the process of constant review.

Us | Chapter 005 Human Nature and Divinity

Was human nature inherently good, or inherently evil? Actually, neither is it. Human genes determine a person’s physical condition to a certain extent, and that physical condition, in turn, determines a person’s emotions to some degree. But this is not yet elevated to the level of good or evil. The true force that prompts humans to make good or evil decisions, beyond the cognition that humans themselves acquire, is a condition—the environment. The surrounding environment, the external people and things, and the interactive process between them and the subject, determine what experience the subject receives, which in turn determines the kind of reaction the subject will make. Good and evil are merely momentary impulses.

Us | Chapter 008 The Fallacy of Knowledge

The three-dimensional visual image that humans see on a screen is not a true three-dimensional visual image.

In a real world, a genuine scene comprises various physical objects, but within a mathematical model, these objects can be perfect, continuously differentiable curves and surfaces described by equations. The problem is that perfect continuity and infinite differentiability are nearly impossible to implement in engineering. Therefore, in genuine practice, engineering fields break down real-world data models into computable modules. For instance, an object in a real scene might be represented as a dense collection of spatial points, known as a point cloud; it could be represented by lines and surfaces made of three-dimensional vertices, or a mesh; or it might be a spatial volume constructed by stacking fundamental geometric bodies, known as a voxel. Regardless of the specific representation method, such decomposition makes the engineering realization feasible.

Us | Chapter 009 The Significance of Symbols

Before the invention of symbols, human information could only be transmitted orally at specific times and in specific places. It wasn’t until the invention of ideograms and pictograms, followed by the standardization and systematization of writing, that human civilization began to transcend time and space through written records and transmission.

The form of human writing systems is primarily used for semantic representation or phonological representation. Although different writing systems cannot be absolutely classified as purely semantic or purely phonological as they develop, their main characteristics are evident. For instance, logographic scripts, represented by Chinese characters and ancient Egyptian script, feature characters that are independent concepts (even if some characters combine sound and meaning). Such writing is highly condensed; even short combinations can carry massive amounts of information. However, most writing systems are primarily phonetic. For example, the Latinate system—represented by French, Spanish, Italian, and Portuguese—, the Germanic system—represented by English, German, and Dutch—, and the Slavic system—represented by Russian, Ukrainian, and Polish—all utilize alphabetical character systems. Every character in an alphabetical system represents an indivisible, completely independent phoneme: some are vowels, and some are consonants. These characters are then combined according to specific rules to form words, and only then do the words acquire meaning. However, phonetic character systems have other branches. For instance, the consonantal alphabet systems, represented by Arabic and Hebrew, use letters to record the most distinctive consonants of pronunciation, primarily aiding recitation and spoken memory. Vowels, meanwhile, require a separate, complex set of rules for automatic completion. As for the phonetic characters in the Sino-Tibetan language family, Japanese and Korean both use syllable systems, where a character is a combination of a consonant and a vowel. Thai, however, uses a combination of a consonant, a vowel, and a tone.
