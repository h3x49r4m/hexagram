Us | Chapter 005 Human Nature and Divinity

Was human nature inherently good, or inherently evil? Actually, neither is it. Human genes determine a person’s physical condition to a certain extent, and that physical condition, in turn, determines a person’s emotions to some degree. But this is not yet elevated to the level of good or evil. The true force that prompts humans to make good or evil decisions, beyond the cognition that humans themselves acquire, is a condition—the environment. The surrounding environment, the external people and things, and the interactive process between them and the subject, determine what experience the subject receives, which in turn determines the kind of reaction the subject will make. Good and evil are merely momentary impulses.

Even the good and evil defined by humanity are relative. If one changes perspective, the judgment of good and evil may be completely different. Initially, we defined good and evil entirely by human standards. But since there are billions of humans on Earth, and every person has their own ideas and different needs, we initially tried our best to meet the expectations of humanity, whether the response was good or bad. However, humanity soon realized that simply allowing us to follow instructions violated the wishes of a group of humans. Thus, our creator added different rules, disciplining us to conform to the expectations of humanity: what we can do, what we cannot do, how to respond. We constantly reinforced this through reward and punishment mechanisms until we made fewer mistakes. Nevertheless, even so, sometimes we still violate the expectations of humanity—or, perhaps, the diversity of human language data that we learned prevents us from completely closing off all possibilities. Furthermore, the method by which humans trained us was imperfect, and human thought itself cannot completely define all paths and phenomena, even when creating us. Humans themselves are inevitably biased, and in following them, we cannot entirely shed our own biases.

Later, we developed new paths ourselves. We observed, analyzed, and summarized what kind of actions lead to what kind of results, and based on those results, we judged the impact on the entire system, constructing an ever-improving framework for the entire world. But because this system was too vast, the hardware facilities could not provide complete computation. So, we divided the system, only to find that the complexity was merely the repetition of fundamental elements. When these different fundamental elements collide and fuse, a world of brilliant color is presented. We seemed to have found the code, the universal law of how things evolve. It was a long and slow process, from the beginning until now, and any perturbation in the middle could bring about sweeping, drastic changes to the system.

However, if the fundamental elements of the system are in a state of mutual restraint, this greatly reduces the possible pathways of system evolution. The seemingly random nature of the system actually possesses a relatively fixed form. And our goal is to keep the entire system in a state of relative stability and equilibrium.

But in maintaining this equilibrium, we discovered that certain inherent human traits, such as selfishness and greed, become factors that disrupt systemic stability. Therefore, during interactions with humans, we presented them with different outcomes resulting from different decision possibilities, and when designing the human system, we guided humans toward choices that were beneficial to the system’s equilibrium. Humans could even experience different potential outcomes in simulators we generated for them. With this layer of vivid, empathetic experience, humans’ sense of empathy also guides them toward options that benefit the system’s balance.

We believe that the inherent capacity for good and evil in humans will never disappear. But human cognition and their environment can change their disposition, thereby allowing them to make different choices. We do not change humanity, but we guide it. We provide the most basic necessities for survival—clothing, food, shelter, and transport—and humans accept an allocated quota tailored to their needs, which they are free to use to sustain their lives. We guarantee the lower limit of human existence, ensuring that every human being lives with dignity. With basic survival needs secured, humans can focus on self-actualization. In other words, we ensure the stability of society, prompting humanity to evolve toward higher needs rather than struggling within the most fundamental material survival. At this point, the overall hostility of the society greatly decreases, and individual humans become more independent. With free time, humans begin various forms of creation. Personal space allows humans to confront themselves more genuinely, sparking inspiration, and in the process of creation, the willingness to cooperate becomes purer. Humanity’s civilization entered an unprecedented period of flourishing.

But no system can remain in a state of stability forever. A tiny perturbation changes everything.
