Chapter 1 The Proterozoic Eon

The Proterozoic Eon is considered the most primitive and perhaps the most dramatic period in Earth’s history. Due to the lack of direct paleogeological records, hypotheses about this period must be derived and proposed through the cross-comparison and comprehensive analysis of the earliest terrestrial minerals, lunar materials, and ancient meteorite samples.

Analysis of these samples reveals that their elemental and mineral compositions not only exhibit enormous spatial heterogeneity (non-uniform mixing of materials) but also show systematic non-equilibrium in their isotopic distribution (meaning the ratios of isotopes greatly exceed the range achievable in any calm, stable environment). This suggests that these elements underwent instantaneous, overloaded chemical reactions under conditions of ultra-high temperature and ultra-high pressure in extremely short periods.

If material evolution were a steady, gradual process, it would be impossible to continuously and massively discover traces of such violent material reorganization. Based on this observational conclusion, mainstream scientific models hypothesize that such a process could only physically occur under a unique environment—namely, when the Earth’s surface was covered by a massive, highly mixed, high-energy semi-molten material layer (a magma ocean).

If this hypothesis holds true, the probability of life originating in such an extreme, scorching, and rapidly changing early environment was extremely low. However, this intense process of material transformation released huge amounts of thermal energy, providing the necessary energy source for the formation of the early atmosphere. This, in turn, facilitated the accumulation of massive amounts of critical volatile gases, such as water vapor and carbon dioxide, thereby laying the material foundation for subsequent geological evolution and the emergence of life.
