Preface

Reconstructing the evolutionary history of Earth is a monumental undertaking. Life comes and goes, but the traces of existence never entirely vanish.

From the subtle clues of rocks and fossils, we glimpse the journey of life.

The primary methods for tracing life’s history involve several fields of study: Analyzing the mineral composition, formation processes, and depositional structures of rocks allows us to infer the ancient environments, energy levels, and chemical conditions when sedimentary rocks formed. Analyzing the type, diversity, structure, and evolutionary sequence of fossils helps us reconstruct the ecological niches, survival pressures, food chains, and environmental adaptability of ancient organisms. Furthermore, precisely measuring elements or isotopes within samples (such as rocks, sediments, and fossil bones) helps us reconstruct Earth’s temperature, water cycle, seawater salinity, and atmospheric composition.

This is a process of continuous refinement and correction. Whenever new rock or fossil samples emerge—whether they corroborate past theories or contradict them—they may either update or completely overturn existing knowledge.

According to the geological timeline, units of time progress from Eon, to Era, to Period, to Epoch, and finally to Age. If we use Eons as the unit, Earth can be divided into two major stages: the Pre-Cambrian Eon and the Phanerozoic Eon. Their greatest difference lies in the record itself: the Pre-Cambrian Eon lacks sufficient geological documentation, meaning earlier periods rely heavily on indirect observation and conceptual modeling. In contrast, the Phanerozoic Eon boasts massive amounts of rock and fossil evidence detailing the emergence and change of species.

Within the Pre-Cambrian Eon, there are the Proterozoic, Paleozoic, and Archaean Eons. The Phanerozoic Eon, due to its abundance of evidence, allows for a more detailed view across the Paleozoic Era, the Mesozoic Era, and the Cenozoic Era. The Paleozoic Era is subdivided into the Cambrian, Ordovician, Silurian, Devonian, Carboniferous, and Permian Periods. The Mesozoic Era is divided into the Triassic, Jurassic, and Cretaceous Periods. Finally, the Cenozoic Era is divided into the Paleogene, Neogene, and Quaternary Periods.

Throughout each of these periods, Earth’s atmosphere and life have undergone dramatic fluctuations and magnificent evolutions. From the original marine ecosystems to terrestrial civilizations, from the earliest single-celled life to intelligent life possessing complex social structures and self-awareness—this has been a journey of continuous restarts and evolution. Geology, atmosphere, and life have interacted profoundly, subjecting life to the cyclical rhythms of “Great Explosions,” “Great Expansions,” and “Great Extinctions.” Yet, it is precisely this series of periodic fluctuations and colossal changes that facilitated life’s ultimate transcendence and leap forward.
