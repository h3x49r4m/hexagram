Chapter 2 The Archean Eon

The most distinctive geological record of the Archean Eon is the Greenstone Belt.

Greenstone Belts are widely distributed in many ancient rock layers across the globe, serving as the ancient foundation of various continental plates. The mineral composition of these belts is extremely diverse, forming a complex aggregate of rocks deposited through multiple geological events and periods.

In terms of lithology, they can be grouped into three main types: igneous rocks, metamorphic rocks, and sedimentary rocks.

1. Igneous Rock Assemblage: These primarily consist of materials such as basalt, andesite, and pillow lavas, typically forming at the edges of oceanic rifts or subduction zones. They are crucial physical evidence documenting intense magmatic activity within the crust, recording the traces of magma expulsion during continental stretching and thinning.

2. Metamorphic Rock Assemblage: This includes high-stress rocks like gneiss, slate, and quartzite, usually formed in convergence zones where tectonic blocks collide. These rocks result from deep burial and massive structural compression, marking a period when the Earth underwent continental amalgamation.

3. Sedimentary Rock Assemblage: Composed primarily of materials such as mudstone, carbonate rock, and sandstone, these rocks typically form in ancient, stable shallow or deep marine basins. This assemblage constitutes the core record of Earth’s biological chronology.

Within the sedimentary rock assemblage of the Archean Eon, alongside basic depositional material accumulation, we find evidence of microfossils or microstructures, stromatolites, and banded iron formations.

Microfossils are fossil remains or traces of ancient organisms that are extremely small and difficult to observe directly with the naked eye (e.g., bacteria, ancient cyanobacteria). They represent the earliest chemical energy metabolism of life and constitute the initial, most primitive evidence of life’s activity.

Microstructures are imprints left by life processes. Examples include minute structures formed when microorganisms moved, preyed, or secreted metabolic byproducts within sediments. These fossils generally contain residual organic carbon, biomarkers, and fossilized clays.

  • Residual Organic Carbon: This is the carbonized evidence of complex organic polymers (such as lipids or protein fragments) remaining after the dissolution of biological cell structures.
  • Biomarkers: These are unique chemical molecular structures left by fossils. These molecules allow scientists to infer the metabolic pathways, nutritional types, and environmental conditions of ancient organisms.
  • Fossil Clays: These serve as the matrix that encapsulates and preserves these life processes.

Stromatolites are cyclic, organized depositional structures. They formed because living organisms periodically altered the chemical state of their surrounding environment (such as local pH values, oxygen concentrations, or nutrient accumulation) during processes of growth, attachment, mucus secretion, and particle capture. This cyclical biological activity and sedimentary encapsulation eventually formed distinct, layered structures within the rock layer. They represent the evolution of life from individual organisms to advanced communities. The composition of stromatolites is multiphasic and shows clear periodicity, containing concentrated minerals, organic enrichment zones, and clay minerals.

  • Concentrated Minerals: These primarily consist of original minerals found in the sediment, such as quartz and calcite, which were captured and stabilized by the organisms.
  • Organic Enrichment Zones: These are layered structures within different periods of cyclicity that concentrate large amounts of biogenic organic carbon, representing the remnants of biological growth and metabolism.
  • Clay Minerals: These acted as the critical material for periodic deposition and encapsulation, recording fluctuations in environmental parameters.

Banded Iron Formations (BIFs) represent the most direct and macroscopic record of global chemical cycle changes in Earth’s history. They mark the critical transition moment when Earth’s global chemical environment shifted from a reduced state (iron-rich) to an oxidized state. BIFs have clear alternating band structures, mainly containing two chemical components: iron-rich layers and silica-rich or manganese-rich layers.

  • Iron-Rich Layers: These are the core of the BIFs, primarily composed of iron oxides or iron hydroxides, with representative minerals being hematite, magnetite, or various iron hydrates. They provide evidence that the global iron cycle reached a critical point, leading to large-scale, unbalanced precipitation of iron.
  • Silica-rich or Manganese-rich Layers: These layered materials serve as the boundaries and mediums alternating with the iron deposits. Representative minerals are silicon dioxide and manganese dioxide. They record environmental parameters during the precipitation process of iron minerals, such as whether the silicates were supersaturated.

The co-existence of microfossils/microstructures, stromatolites, and BIFs is not coincidental; they are connected by a causal chain representing a massive change in the chemical cycle:

  1. Microfossils/Microstructures represent the earliest form of chemical energy metabolism of life.
  2. Over time, these life forms developed the organizational capacity of communities, leading to the formation of Stromatolites—structures with periodic, layered organization within the rock strata.
  3. This biological advance ultimately triggered a global chemical upheaval, leaving behind the characteristic Banded Iron Formations.

Therefore, based on the structure of Greenstone Belt rocks, we can deduce the general evolutionary process of Earth during the Archean Eon:

In the early Archean, Earth was a tectonically active environment, rich in materials but characterized by a highly reduced chemistry. Volcanic eruptions built a continuously changing crust, while the atmosphere lacked oxygen, filled instead with reducing gases. Life emerged in the form of primitive prokaryotes. These organisms were structurally simple and lacked complex nuclear structures. They performed the initial synthesis of life matter by capturing chemical energy released from redox reactions between reducing substances in the environment (like hydrogen sulfide) and oxidizers (like trivalent iron ions). They utilized this energy to chemically modify carbon dioxide (the raw material) into organic molecules with high energy potential—molecules that could be directly utilized by life. During this phase, Earth’s life energy was driven by chemical energy.

Among these single-celled prokaryotes, some bacteria and archaea (such as cyanobacteria) could secrete bioglue, connecting individual bacteria into dense, elevated biological mats. As the bacterial community grew, external sediments continuously accumulated atop this attached biological mat. These accumulated sediments were not only attached but also, over time and under geological pressure, formed mineral cement between the biological glue and the minerals, eventually becoming structurally stable stromatolites. The appearance of stromatolites proves that life evolved from individual organisms to highly collaborative communities.

The structure of the bacterial colony not only ensured its stability against continuous disturbances from currents and tides but also ensured that it could capture sunlight over large areas for extended periods. It used captured light energy as an energy source, converting inorganic carbon dioxide and water into high-energy organic carbon through photosynthesis, while continuously releasing large amounts of oxygen molecules. When these oxygen molecules encountered dissolved ferrous iron ions, the iron was oxidized and precipitated as insoluble iron oxyhydroxides and other iron minerals. These precipitates accumulated in the sedimentary layers, forming the characteristically periodic, alternating banded iron formations. The existence of BIFs serves as the indirect proof of the presence of oxygen in the early Earth’s atmosphere.

As time and deposition accumulated, the ferrous iron ions available for oxygen reactions in the water were completely depleted, and massive amounts of oxygen entered the atmospheric cycle, finally initiating Earth’s “Great Oxidation Event.” The Great Oxidation Event was not merely a quantitative increase in oxygen, but a qualitative change in redox potential. It meant that Earth’s environment transitioned from highly reduced (dominated by reducing gases) to highly oxidized (dominated by oxygen). For life forms that were only adapted to reducing environments, oxygen became a fatal poison, greatly restricting their habitat. However, this fatal chemical pressure, in turn, became the ultimate engine driving the evolution of life. It compelled life forms to evolve defense systems against oxygen toxicity and ultimately master and utilize oxygen as the final electron acceptor in aerobic respiration. This metabolic shift dramatically increased the efficiency of energy capture, ultimately pushing Earth’s life from simple prokaryotic life toward complex, differentiated eukaryotic life, completing the grand leap of species.
